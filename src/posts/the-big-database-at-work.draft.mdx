At LinkedIn, my team runs a [multi-primary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-master_replication) MySQL system with roughly 20 billion rows across six tables
and 15 TB of stored data. The tables aren't partitioned, and the system isn't sharded. Yet, our queries are still _suprisingly fast_.

<BigDatabaseRegionsFigure />

<BigDatabaseHardwareFigure />

Yes, it helps that we run some of the largest MySQL instances available at 32 Cores, 256GB Memory, and 24TB disk. But why is it still fast? We see about 10,000 SELECTs per second on average across the three regions, with a reported
average query time of 0.48 milliseconds as we mainly serve point lookups or search on indexed fields. The buffer pool for our database is sized at 100 GB
and its reported hit rate is 98.7%!

<BigDatabaseTrafficFigure />

Since the IDs for our rows increase with time, new entries are written nearby each other. Recent rows tend to occupy nearby pages. Repeated reads make those pages hot and useful to keep in the buffer pool; older history is rarely retrieved in this workload.

In essence, this workload serves "hot" data where most pages are served from memory (in the buffer pool), so we rarely need to read pages from disk. New data stays in memory until it "ages out" and is no longer fetched.
